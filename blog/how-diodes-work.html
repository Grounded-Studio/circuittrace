How diodes work: one-way arrows in flow puzzles.

📅 16 November 2026 · ⏱ 5 min read · ✍️ Grounded Studio

A diode is the simplest extra rule in Circuit Trace, and one of the strongest. It's a cell with an arrow on it. It looks like a restriction, but once you know how to read it, an arrow is mostly a gift: it tells you where a line goes next.

The rule

A line may enter a diode from any side, but it must leave in the direction the arrow points. That's all. The arrow says nothing about where the line comes from, only where it goes after the diode cell.

Two details are worth knowing:

  • Neutral diodes (white arrows) let any colour through.
  • Coloured diodes only accept the line of their own colour. Every other colour is stopped before it can enter.

A diode never sits on a dot, so a line always passes through a diode rather than starting or ending on it.

Six tips for reading arrows

1. The cell the arrow points at is almost decided

Whatever line uses the diode continues into the next cell in the arrow's direction. If you know which colour goes through the diode, you already know two cells of its route.

2. Only the exit is fixed

The most common mistake is treating an arrow like a corridor. A line can come in from behind the arrow or from either side. Keep all three entries in mind.

3. An arrow pointing at a dot

If an arrow points straight at a dot, the line through that diode will very often be that dot's colour, arriving at the end of its route. Try that first.

4. Arrows near the edge

An arrow that points along the edge of the board pushes its line along the rim, where there's little room to turn. Follow it a few cells and see where it is forced to go.

5. Coloured diodes are signposts

A coloured diode tells you that this colour passes through this cell, and in which direction it leaves. That's often enough to sketch most of the route between its two dots.

6. On fill levels, every arrow is used

When every cell has to be filled, no diode can be skipped. Some line has to go through each one, in its direction. Ask "who can reach this arrow from a legal side?" and you often get a single answer.

A worked example

Picture a 6×6 board with a white arrow in the middle of the top row, pointing down. Which line uses it? It can't be a line running along the top edge from left to right, because that line would have to turn down at the arrow and leave the edge. So the line through the arrow arrives from the left or the right (there is nothing above it) and then heads down into the board. Now look at the cell below the arrow: it belongs to the same line. Two cells decided before you've drawn anything, and the top row is split into a left half and a right half that other colours have to deal with separately. That is how arrows work: one small rule, several free moves.

Placing diodes in the editor

In the Puzzle Editor, pick the ▶ DIODE tool and tap a cell. Tap the same diode again to rotate it: right, down, left, up, and one more tap removes it. To make a coloured diode, pick a colour first and then place the diode. The editor won't let you put a diode on a dot. Read the full walkthrough in how to make your own flow puzzle.

Frequently asked questions

What does a diode do in Circuit Trace?

It is a one-way cell. A line can enter it from any side, but it has to leave in the direction of the arrow.

What is the difference between a coloured and a white diode?

A white (neutral) diode lets any colour through. A coloured diode only lets the line of its own colour through.

Can a line end on a diode?

No. A diode never sits on a dot, so a line always passes through it.
