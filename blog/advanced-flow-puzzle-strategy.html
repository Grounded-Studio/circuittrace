Advanced flow puzzle strategy: 8 techniques for the hardest boards.

📅 16 November 2026 · ⏱ 6 min read · ✍️ Grounded Studio

You know the basics: start at the edges, make the forced moves, never leave a dead end. If not, start with our beginner guide to flow puzzles. This one is for the boards where those tips run out: 9×9 and 10×10 grids, eight or more colours, and levels where every cell has to be filled.

Every technique below is a way of asking the same question: what can't be true? On a hard board you rarely see the right move directly. You see the wrong ones, and the right move is what is left.

1. Read the empty regions, not the lines

Once a few lines are down, the board splits into empty regions. Look at each one as its own small puzzle. Which dots are inside it? Which lines can still get in, and through how many openings? A region with no dots and no way in is already a mistake. Spotting that early saves you from finishing a line and only then noticing that a corner can never be reached.

2. The one-door pocket rule

A pocket of empty cells with only one opening can't be filled by a line passing through, because a passing line needs a way in and a way out. So on a fill-every-cell level, a one-door pocket must contain a line end: a dot whose line starts or finishes inside it. If no dot is inside, the move that created the pocket was wrong. This rule alone solves a surprising number of "I'm stuck" moments.

3. Corners decide themselves

An empty corner cell has only two neighbours. Any line that fills it has to use both. The moment one of those two neighbours is taken by a line that can't turn into the corner, the corner is dead, unless a dot sits in it. Work through the corners after every few moves; they are the cheapest cells on the board to check.

4. Never separate a pair

Before you commit a long line, check every other colour: can its two dots still reach each other? A line that cuts the board in two, with one blue dot on each side, can't be part of the solution. On big boards this is the most common hidden mistake, because the separating line often looks perfectly reasonable on its own.

5. Settle bottlenecks by asking who has no choice

When two colours both want the same one-cell gap, don't pick the one that looks nicer. Ask which colour has another route. The colour without an alternative gets the gap, and the other one has to go around. If neither has an alternative, an earlier move is wrong.

6. Use the fill rule as a weapon

On levels where every cell must be filled, empty space is not free space. A long corridor along the edge has to be used by somebody. Count which lines could reach it. If only one colour can, that colour goes there, even if its dots are right next to each other. Short, lazy connections are often wrong on fill levels for exactly this reason.

7. Long pairs first, short pairs last

Pairs whose dots are far apart have the fewest sensible routes once the board fills up, so they constrain everything else. Short pairs can usually squeeze in at the end. When you're unsure where to start, sketch the route of the longest pair around the outside and see what it leaves behind.

8. Test a move, then follow it a few steps

Sometimes you have to try something. Do it with a plan: make one move, then follow only the moves it forces, three or four steps deep. If that chain ends in a dead end, a separated pair or a sealed pocket, the move was wrong and the other option is right. Undo and take it. This is logic, not guessing: you are proving a move false. Mechanics change the questions

  • Diodes fix the direction a line leaves a cell, which turns many open questions into forced moves. See how diodes work.
  • Portals make two distant cells neighbours, so a pocket can have a hidden door. See how portals work.
  • A cube has no edges or corners, so rules 2 and 3 matter less and region reading matters more. See solving 3D cube puzzles.

Frequently asked questions

What makes a flow puzzle hard?

Board size, the number of colours and the rule that every cell must be filled. Hard puzzles have few forced moves at the start, so you have to reason about what cannot be true.

Is it cheating to try moves?

No. Trying a move and following what it forces is a normal logical technique. The goal is to prove a move wrong, not to stumble on the answer.

How do I know if a region is a dead end?

Count its openings and the dots inside it. A region without dots needs at least two openings for a line to pass through and fill it.
