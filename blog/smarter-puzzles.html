Why our puzzles got harder, and why that's a good thing.

📅 5 November 2026 · ⏱ 6 min read · ✍️ Grounded Studio

While testing the last sectors of the campaign this autumn, the verdict was blunt. Three screenshots, one note: "At this point it should be really hard. But the colours are all right next to each other. One glance and I already know what to do."

That note was right. And it wasn't one or two levels. It was most of them.

What was wrong

We looked at the levels the way a player does, and the pattern was easy to see. Each colour's two dots sat close together, and the path between them was short and obvious. On a big board with eight colours, that turns into eight little puzzles side by side instead of one big one. Bigger, but not harder.

Worse, many boards could be filled in lots of different ways. When a puzzle has many answers, almost anything you try works. That feels nice for a minute, and then it feels like nothing.

Teaching a computer to think like a player

To fix it, we first needed a way to measure it. "Hard" is a feeling, so we built a solver that solves puzzles the way people do, in three steps:

  • See. Moves you can make without thinking. A dot with only one free neighbour has to go there. A corner cell has to be the end of a line or a bend.
  • Think. "What if this line went left?" Try it in your head, and if it leads to a dead end, you know it goes the other way. Every time the solver needs a step like this, the level gets a little harder.
  • Guess. When even thinking ahead doesn't settle it, you have to try something and back out if it fails. This counts the heaviest.

The solver adds that up into one score. A level it can finish just by seeing scores close to zero. That's the "one glance" puzzle from that note. A level that needs a dozen "what if" moments scores much higher.

Rebuilding the packs

With a way to measure, we went through everything:

  • Classic, Diodes, Portals and Arcade were rebuilt from scratch, 100 levels each. Every sector got a target score, and the levels were picked to hit it, so each sector is a real step up from the last.
  • The main campaign kept its tutorials exactly as they were. The other 86 levels got new boards with the same size, the same number of colours and the same mechanics, but rated by the solver.
  • Labyrinth, the new pack with shaped boards and walls, was built this way from the start. More about Labyrinth here.
  • Daily, Weekly, Endless and Time Trial now rate their puzzles too, right on your phone, before you see them. Endless and Time Trial keep it quick, so you never wait.

💡 What this means when you're stuck. Most levels now have one clear answer, and the solver could reach it by reasoning. So if a level feels like it needs a lucky guess, it doesn't. Somewhere on the board there's a cell with only one option. Look at corners, dead ends and narrow passages first.

A correction

In an earlier post we wrote about handcrafting 500 puzzles. That's how the game started, and it's why that note stung a little. Our hands made levels that looked good, but a lot of them didn't make you think.

The puzzles are now picked by the solver, and we tune the targets by hand: how hard each sector should be, where it can ramp up, and where a breather fits. We think that's a better split: the computer does the measuring, and we decide what's fun.

Was it worth it?

When the new levels went back into testing, the note changed to: "The difficulty is so much better." That's the review we were hoping for.

If you finished Circuit Trace before, it's worth another look. The level numbers are the same, but the boards behind them are new.
