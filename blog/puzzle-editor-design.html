How we designed the Puzzle Editor.

📅 15 October 2026 · ⏱ 7 min read · ✍️ Grounded Studio

The Puzzle Editor started as a bug report. A friend playtesting an early build sent me a screenshot of level 47 and asked, "could I make one of these for my brother?"

Three weeks later it shipped. Here's how.

The core bet

Most mobile puzzle games treat user-generated content as a feature for the top 1% of players. Big import/export menus, cloud accounts, moderation queues, a dedicated "Community" tab. All of it designed by committee and nobody uses it.

We wanted the opposite: a thing a casual player could try in 60 seconds without opening a tutorial. The success metric wasn't "how many puzzles get uploaded" — it was "how many share codes get pasted into WhatsApp."

That reframing killed a lot of ideas before we built them. No upvotes, no profiles, no comments. No server. The whole editor is a direct line from one person's brain to another person's phone.

Week 1: the grid

First version was embarrassingly thin: pick a grid size (5×5 to 8×8), tap a cell to drop a coloured dot, tap another to pair it. That's it.

What surprised me is how long it took to make that feel right. Specifically:

  • Tap targets. Phone grids need huge touch zones. The editor cell is bigger than a real play cell, with a 20% invisible margin that still registers taps. Nobody notices until you remove it and tapping suddenly feels bad.
  • Palette memory. The editor remembers your last colour and places on first tap. Second tap pairs. Third tap starts a new pair in the same colour. Tiny thing, saves roughly half your interactions.
  • The CLEAR button is scary. First version wiped the whole grid instantly. First playtester did it by accident, lost their puzzle, uninstalled nothing but was clearly annoyed. Now CLEAR shows a tiny confirmation ("Clear this puzzle?") with a 2-second undo window.

Week 2: solvability

This is where the editor gets interesting — and where cheap imitations fall apart. Anyone can let you place dots. The hard part is telling you whether the puzzle is actually solvable before you share it with a friend.

Circuit Trace has a real solver under the hood — it's the same one that validates the 500 hand-crafted campaign puzzles. We just exposed it to the editor. Hit the VALIDATE button and within 100ms you get one of three answers:

  • ✅ "Looks solvable — playtest to confirm" — the solver found at least one valid path arrangement.
  • ⚠️ "No solution — pair X and Y block each other" — explicit conflict identified. The two blocking pairs blink red for a second.
  • ❓ "Takes too long to verify — try fewer pairs" — the solver timed out at 2 seconds. We don't ship a maybe-unsolvable puzzle to a friend.

The "playtest to confirm" wording matters. A solver can prove a puzzle is technically solvable without proving it's fun. So we added a TEST button right next to VALIDATE: play your own creation inside the editor before you share it. If you can't solve it in 3 minutes, maybe your friend can't either.

Week 3: the share flow

The last week was almost entirely about the SHARE button. Specifically: what happens after you tap it.

Version 1 generated a code and copied it to the clipboard with a tiny toast: "Code copied!" That was functional and nobody shared anything with it. Why? Because "copied to clipboard" means an extra app-switch, an extra paste, an extra context explanation to your friend.

Version 2 (the shipped one) opens the OS share sheet directly with a pre-written message:

"I made a Circuit Trace puzzle for you — try it: CT-NHH06-8CMY4-AG2DS. Get the game: https://circuittrace.app"

Pick WhatsApp, pick a contact, send. Three taps instead of six. The share rate roughly tripled in A/B playtesting.

What got cut

Three features that almost shipped:

  • Portals in the editor. Technically ready. Cut because 90% of first-time editor users were already overwhelmed with just dots + diodes. We'll add it in a future update once the base editor has social proof.
  • A gallery of community puzzles. Would have required a server, moderation, abuse reports, and GDPR consent. All real work for a feature that competes with the "share with your actual friend" use case we were optimising for.
  • Difficulty rating. Players would have gamed it instantly ("mine is 10/10 hardest!"). Instead the receiving player just sees grid size + pair count — raw facts, no bragging.

What shipped

5×5 to 8×8 grids. Dots + diodes. A real solver for live validation. A 5-slot library so you can save your best creations. Share codes in the CT-XXXXX-XXXXX-XXXXX format that fits in a WhatsApp message. The TEST button so you can play your own puzzle before sending it.

No account. No cloud. No gallery. Just you, a grid, and someone you want to send it to.

Three weeks of work, and it's quietly become the feature our beta testers mention most.
